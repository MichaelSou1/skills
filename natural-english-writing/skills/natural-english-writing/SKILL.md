# Natural English Writing

Write for a reader who needs to follow the argument, not decode compressed phrasing. Apply the following principles when drafting or revising; fix actual sources of friction rather than mechanically eliminating constructions.

- **Keep related words close.** When a long insertion separates a subject from its main verb or a modifier from what it describes, move the insertion or split the sentence. Sentence length alone is not the problem.
- **Unpack crowded noun strings.** When several nouns are stacked and their relationships are unclear, make those relationships explicit with a verb or preposition. Keep familiar terms intact: for example, change “visual model training reward design” to “the design of rewards for training a visual model,” while retaining an established term such as “reinforcement learning.”
- **Keep helpful connecting words.** In a complex object-relative clause, include an optional “that” or “which” when it helps the reader see the structure. Do not add one to every simple phrase such as “the results we report.”
- **Use familiar verbs.** Replace an improvised verb made from a noun when a conventional phrase describes the action more clearly. Established verbs such as “benchmark” need no automatic replacement.
- **Make actions concrete.** When a metaphor gives an inanimate thing an unusually human action and obscures the mechanism, state the action directly. For example, “this asymmetry bites” may be clearer as “this asymmetry causes problems” when that is what the context means. Ordinary scientific expressions such as “the results show” and “the model learns” are natural.
- **Prefer natural word combinations.** Replace awkward, rare collocations with familiar, precise phrasing. Low-frequency combinations are not inherently wrong; retain established technical terminology and unusual wording that conveys a necessary distinction.

Preserve the author's intended meaning, evidence, numbers, uncertainty, technical distinctions, citations, voice, and requested scope. Do not add claims or replace precise terminology with vaguer synonyms. Keep local edits local, and leave quoted text and code unchanged unless the user asks to modify them. Deliver the requested text without an audit of these principles unless the user asks for one.

The principles are informed by [Clark, Martin, and Mahowald, *Why is AI-generated language so hard to understand?* (2026), §3.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.29162v1#S3.SS1). The paper studies English-language features associated with processing difficulty; these features are editing cues, not proof of AI authorship.
